German has two words for "you", and choosing between them is the first social
decision a learner makes in almost every conversation. **Sie** is the formal
address, **du** the informal one. Getting it wrong is rarely offensive, but it
is immediately noticeable, in the way that calling a stranger by their first
name is noticeable in English.

The reliable default with an adult you do not know is **Sie**, together with
their surname: *Guten Tag, Frau Weber.* This holds for shop assistants, doctors,
officials, landlords, and almost anyone over about twenty-five whom you are
meeting in a professional context.

---

At work the picture varies more than learners expect. Traditional firms,
public administration and most of the trades keep **Sie** between colleagues who
have worked together for years. Startups, agencies, universities and much of
the tech industry use **du** from the first day, and using **Sie** there can read
as stiff rather than polite. Listen to how two colleagues address each other
before you address either of them.

The switch from **Sie** to **du** is offered, not assumed. The older person, the
more senior person, or a woman speaking to a man traditionally makes the offer:
*Wollen wir uns duzen?* Accepting is simply *Gerne* — and from that point there
is no going back. If nobody offers, keep using **Sie** indefinitely; a great many
German colleagues do exactly that for a whole career.

---

Two situations override all of this. Anyone under about eighteen is **du**, and
so is anyone in a context that is explicitly informal: sports clubs, hobby
groups, hostels, and nearly all of the internet. Some large retailers — IKEA is
the standard example — use **du** with every customer as company policy.

If you are genuinely unsure, you can avoid the pronoun altogether for a
sentence or two. *Ist hier noch frei?* commits to nothing while you work out
which room you are in.
